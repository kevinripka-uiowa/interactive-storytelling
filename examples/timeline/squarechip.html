Win May 1900

Passage of the Lacey Act

Signed into law by President William McKinley in May 1900, the Lacey Act became the first U.S. federal law to protect wildlife by making it a federal crime to transport illegally taken game or birds across state lines. Driven largely by public outrage over commercial hunting for the fashion industry, particularly the feather and plume trade, it severely curtailed the open-market slaughter of wild birds and established a precedent for federal wildlife protection.

Win March 1903

Pelican Island Federal Bird Reservation Established

In March 1903, President Theodore Roosevelt issued an executive order designating Pelican Island off Florida's Atlantic coast as a protected breeding ground for native birds. Created specifically to protect brown pelicans, egrets, and herons from plume hunters, this single act gave birth to the U.S. National Wildlife Refuge System, which today encompasses hundreds of millions of acres dedicated to wildlife preservation.

Loss September 1914

Extinction of the Passenger Pigeon

On September 1, 1914, "Martha," the world's last known Passenger Pigeon, died in captivity at the Cincinnati Zoo. Once the most abundant bird species in North America, numbering an estimated 3 to 5 billion individuals, unregulated commercial hunting, habitat loss from deforestation, and expanding rail networks drove the species to complete extinction in just a few decades. The loss served as a stark, sobering wake-up call for wildlife conservation.

Loss February 1918

Extinction of the Carolina Parakeet

In February 1918, "Incas," the last captive Carolina Parakeet, died at the Cincinnati Zoo, marking the extinction of the only native parrot species in the eastern United States. The species suffered a rapid decline due to extensive forest clearing, agricultural retaliatory shooting by farmers who viewed them as fruit pests, and intensive hunting for their vibrant green and yellow feathers.

Win July 1918

Ratification of the Migratory Bird Treaty Act

Enacted in July 1918 to fulfill a 1916 treaty between the United States and Great Britain on behalf of Canada, the Migratory Bird Treaty Act made it illegal to pursue, hunt, take, capture, or kill migratory birds, or trade their feathers, eggs, or nests, without a federal permit. It remains one of the strongest legal safeguards for over 1,000 avian species across North America.

Win June 1972

EPA Bans Domestic Agricultural Use of DDT

Following years of public advocacy spearheaded by Rachel Carson's 1962 book Silent Spring, the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency banned agricultural use of the synthetic pesticide DDT in June 1972. DDT had contaminated aquatic ecosystems and caused severe eggshell thinning in apex avian predators, causing populations of Bald Eagles, Peregrine Falcons, Ospreys, and Brown Pelicans to crash. The ban ignited massive recoveries across affected species over subsequent decades.

Win December 1973

Passage of the Endangered Species Act

Signed into law by President Richard Nixon in December 1973, the Endangered Species Act established comprehensive protection for imperiled plants and animals and their critical habitats. For birds, the ESA provided the essential federal framework, funding, and critical habitat designations required to launch targeted recovery and reintroduction programs.

Loss / Turning Point April 1987

Capture of the Last Wild California Condor

On April 19, 1987, biologists captured the last remaining wild California Condor, designated "AC-9," in the southern California mountains, bringing the wild population to zero and leaving only 27 individuals alive in captive breeding facilities. While a tragic loss of the bird in its natural environment, this controversial intervention prevented total extinction and allowed intensive breeding programs to rebuild the population, eventually allowing condors to be reintroduced back into the wild.

Win August 2007

Official Delisting of the Bald Eagle

In August 2007, the U.S. Department of the Interior officially removed the Bald Eagle from the federal list of Threatened and Endangered Species in the lower 48 states. After plummeting to fewer than 500 nesting pairs in the 1960s, decades of habitat protection, nest monitoring, and the DDT ban allowed populations to rebound to over 10,000 active breeding pairs by the time of delisting.

Loss / Call to Action September 2019

Publication of the '3 Billion Birds Lost' Study

In September 2019, a landmark study published in Science revealed that North America had lost over 2.9 billion breeding birds since 1970, a net 29% population drop across almost all biomes, including once-abundant generalist species like sparrows, warblers, and blackbirds. The findings highlighted widespread ongoing habitat degradation, agricultural pesticide impacts, and outdoor cat predation, renewing urgent international efforts to update conservation policies.
