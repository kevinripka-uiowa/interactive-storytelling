Choices and Branching Structures

Choice-based interactives let viewers influence what happens next, what information they encounter, or how a system responds. To design them clearly, separate the function of a choice from the experience of making it.

Core distinction: “Moral choice,” “fake choice,” and “resource choice” are not competing categories. They describe different dimensions. One asks what the choice changes; another asks how the choice is experienced.

Two dimensions of choice

Classify a choice in two passes. First identify what changes in the interactive. Then describe how the choice feels to the viewer.

1. What does the choice change?

  1. The next content viewed
  2. The viewer’s expressed position
  3. A path through the narrative
  4. A resource, score, or hidden state
  5. A later consequence or ending

2. How does the choice feel?

  1. Informed or uncertain
  2. Meaningful or weak
  3. Visible or invisible
  4. Expressive or consequential
  5. Moral, strategic, or a dilemma

Example

Choosing whether to repair a car before accepting another rideshare trip may be a resource choice because it changes money, a consequential choice because it affects later outcomes, and a dilemma because every option has a cost.

What the choice changes

These categories describe the choice’s function inside the interactive system. A single choice may fit more than one category.

Navigational choice

Controls which information the viewer sees next without necessarily changing the underlying story.

Example: Choose housing, transportation, or health to explore first.

Interpretive choice

Asks the viewer to make a judgment, prediction, or explanation before receiving evidence or feedback.

Example: Which factor best explains the trend?

Expressive choice

Lets the viewer state an identity, preference, attitude, or style. It may not affect the system much, but it can create ownership.

Example: Choose how your character responds in a conversation.

Branching choice

Sends the viewer to a different scene, passage, or sequence of information.

Example: Verify the tip first or publish immediately.

Resource choice

Changes a limited quantity such as money, time, health, trust, energy, or opportunities.

Example: Spend $200 on repairs or keep the money for rent.

Accumulating choice

Adds to a hidden or visible state that affects later options or outcomes.

Example: Several small credibility decisions determine whether a source trusts you later.

Consequential choice

Changes a later event, condition, relationship, score, or ending.

Example: Skipping maintenance increases the chance of a breakdown later.

Ending choice

Determines or strongly influences the final outcome, often near the end of an otherwise shared narrative.

Example: Publish the investigation or hold it for more reporting.

Classify the function

“You have $40 left. Buy gas so you can keep working, or buy groceries for your family.” Which function is most central?

Choose an answer

The same decision can have several qualities, but one function is most central.

How the choice feels

These categories describe the viewer’s experience of the decision. They can be layered onto any functional choice type.

Informed choice

The viewer has enough information to anticipate likely consequences and make a reasoned decision.

Use when: The purpose is strategy, tradeoffs, or systems thinking.

Unfair choice

The consequences are difficult or impossible to predict from the available information. This can create uncertainty, but it may also frustrate viewers.

Use carefully: It is strongest when uncertainty is itself part of the real-world experience.

Visible choice

The viewer clearly understands that a decision is being made and that the system will respond.

Example: Two labeled buttons with stated tradeoffs.

Invisible choice

The system tracks an action or pattern the viewer may not recognize as a formal decision.

Example: Repeatedly prioritizing speed quietly lowers a trust score.

Meaningful choice

The alternatives produce noticeably different information, states, consequences, or interpretations.

Test: Would the experience change if this choice were removed?

Weak choice

The alternatives technically differ, but the consequences are minor, repetitive, or uninteresting.

Problem: The interface pauses the viewer without adding agency or insight.

Fake choice

Different options lead to essentially the same result while implying that the viewer has meaningful control.

Distinction: A branch that deliberately reconverges is not automatically fake if the path still changes context or state.

Moral choice

Presents alternatives with recognizable ethical implications, often framed as better, worse, or neutral.

Example: Protect a vulnerable source or publish identifying details.

Dilemma

Forces a tradeoff in which every option carries a meaningful cost. There may be no clearly correct answer.

Example: Work while sick or miss a rent payment.

Important distinction

Expressive choice describes what the viewer contributes. Fake choice and weak choice evaluate how much that contribution matters. An expressive choice can be intentionally low-consequence without being deceptive.

Choice quality check

Use these questions to decide whether a choice belongs in the experience.

Clarity: Does the viewer understand what they are deciding?
Difference: Do the alternatives create meaningfully different paths, states, or interpretations?
Information: Does the viewer have an appropriate amount of information for the intended experience?
Consequence: Is the result visible now, accumulated for later, or deliberately hidden?
Purpose: Does making the choice help communicate the subject, or does it only add clicking?
Fairness: When consequences are unpredictable, does that uncertainty reflect the real system being explained?

Branching structures

A branching structure describes how passages, scenes, choices, and outcomes connect. The structures below range from one local split to full narrative systems.

Fork

The simplest branching unit. One choice separates the experience into two or more paths.

Basic building block

Best for

A single comparison, a small scenario, or the first decision in a larger structure. A fork becomes another structure depending on whether paths continue, end, or rejoin.

Time cave

A heavily branching tree in which choices repeatedly create separate timelines with little or no rejoining.

Broad and expensive

Characteristics

  • Many choices have roughly equal structural importance.
  • The number of passages and endings grows rapidly.
  • Earlier decisions remain visible because paths rarely reconverge.

Strength: strong path ownership. Limitation: content multiplies quickly.

Gauntlet

A mostly linear central path with side branches that fail, loop back, or quickly rejoin.

Long, not broad

Characteristics

  • The main sequence stays manageable.
  • Incorrect or risky choices may produce short endings.
  • If multiple major endings exist, they often come from a final choice.

Strength: efficient to build. Risk: repeated failure branches can feel punitive or like a quiz.

Branch and bottleneck

The narrative repeatedly branches, then reconverges around events that occur in every version of the story.

Controlled variation

Characteristics

  • Branches provide different experiences without multiplying forever.
  • Bottlenecks make production manageable.
  • State tracking can preserve effects from earlier choices after paths rejoin.

A reconvergence is not a fake choice when paths change information, state, relationships, or later options.

Quest

A modular structure made of distinct clusters or tasks that can often be approached in different orders before converging on a small number of outcomes.

Modular exploration

Characteristics

  • Each cluster acts like a small local network.
  • Viewers may choose the order of tasks or routes.
  • Paths interconnect within clusters more than between clusters.

This includes the useful idea behind “hub and spoke,” but allows more connection and recombination than a simple menu.

State-based system

Choices change variables such as money, trust, time, health, or reputation. Those states determine later feedback, available choices, and outcomes.

System-driven

Characteristics

  • The same passage can produce different results depending on prior decisions.
  • Some choices may be invisible because the system quietly tracks behavior.
  • The narrative can remain compact while still remembering the viewer’s history.

State-based systems describe logic rather than a single visual shape. They are often layered onto gauntlets, quests, or branch-and-bottleneck narratives.

Choose a structure based on the experience you want and the amount of content and logic you can realistically produce.
