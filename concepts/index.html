Critical Vocabulary

Interactive Media
3P Framework

This interactive guide turns the critical vocabulary deck into a reference page students can click through, revisit, and use while planning story elements.

PurposeWhat should the viewer understand, feel, notice, compare, or explain?
PatternWhat kind of interaction best creates that takeaway?
ProductionHow will the element be built responsibly on the web?
Core rule: Do not start with “I want to make a map.” Start with “What should the viewer understand?” Then choose the form that fits.

Purpose

Purpose defines why the interactive exists. It has three parts: information, takeaway or experience, and story role.

The three parts of purpose

Before choosing a form, identify the material, the desired takeaway, and how the element fits into the larger piece.

Start here

Information

The material your interactive is built from: facts, data, places, people, events, steps, systems, relationships, categories, images, audio, video, or documents.

Takeaway / Experience

What should stick with the viewer after they use it? This could involve scale, change, time, connection, comparison, pattern, distribution, consequence, process, or perspective.

Story Role

Where does the element fit? It might be a sidebar element, an inline element, or the main structure of the story.

Takeaway / Experience

These terms describe what the viewer should understand, feel, notice, or be able to explain after using the interactive.

Purpose vocabulary
How big, far, much, intense, rare, common, or significant something is.
What shifts over time, between conditions, across places, or before and after an event.
When something happens, how long it lasts, what order it follows, or how events sequence across a timeline.
How people, places, causes, systems, events, or categories are linked.
How two or more things are similar or different, including side-by-side contrasts and before/after relationships.
A trend, repetition, cluster, anomaly, or recurring relationship the viewer should notice.
Where something is concentrated, spread out, absent, overrepresented, or unevenly distributed.
Why the information matters and what effects, stakes, risks, or outcomes follow from it.
How something works, what steps it follows, or how parts of a system produce an outcome.
What something feels like, looks like, or means from a specific position, role, location, or point of view.

Story role

Story role asks how the interactive fits into the larger piece. The same interactive form can play different roles depending on where it appears and what the surrounding story needs it to do.

Purpose vocabulary
A sidebar element provides useful extra context next to a related story. It supports the main piece without carrying the full explanation by itself. Use this when the interaction gives background, a reference, a definition set, a small comparison, or optional context.
An inline element appears inside the story flow and helps explain one part of the main story. It should arrive at the moment the viewer needs it. For this course, every interactive you make is treated as an inline element: it should connect directly to the surrounding text, visuals, and explanation.
A main-structure interactive functions as the story itself. The viewer moves through the piece primarily by using the interaction. This role requires stronger organization, clearer instructions, more complete context, and more careful testing because the interactive carries the whole experience.

Inline element planning questions

Because every interactive in this class is treated as an inline element, students should answer these questions before building.

What does it do differently?

What does this element provide that the text, photos, video, audio, or static graphics do not? A strong interactive should add a useful digital capability: revealing, comparing, locating, exploring, explaining, participating, or experiencing.

When does it appear?

Where does this element belong in the story: beginning, middle, or end? What does the viewer need to know before reaching it?

How does it connect?

How does this element connect to the text, visuals, and other interactive elements around it? The headline, deck, instructions, labels, captions, annotations, and surrounding paragraphs should make the connection clear.

Purpose Example: Monarch Migration Route

The same information can lead to several different patterns, depending on what the viewer needs to understand.

Example

Information

Where monarch butterflies travel during migration, including major movement paths between breeding areas in the United States and Canada and overwintering sites in Mexico.

Takeaway / Experience

The viewer should understand that monarch migration is a multi-generational journey spanning thousands of miles. The experience should convey scale, time, connection, consequence, and fragility.

Story role

This element belongs near the beginning of the explainer because it establishes the scale of the migration. Without it, viewers would have a harder time understanding why habitat loss, weather, and conservation across regions matter.

Pattern

Pattern connects the story need to digital media’s capabilities, the viewer’s agency, and the final form.

Affordances

An affordance is what a medium, object, or interface makes possible for a user. For this course, affordances help students describe why an interactive form fits the story need.

Digital capability
Computers can hold and organize far more material than physical media. This supports layered information, databases, archives, annotations, and expandable context.
Digital media can let users move through material in different orders or directions. This supports maps, 360 scenes, pan-and-zoom images, and nonlinear browsing.
A system can execute rules: if this, do this; if that, do that. This supports calculators, quizzes, filters, step logic, animation, and input-driven results.
People can interact with the medium. This supports clicking, choosing, filtering, submitting, comparing, navigating, and shaping what appears next.
Motion is not one of Janet H. Murray’s four main affordances, but it is useful to treat as a practical digital capability. Motion can guide attention, show change over time, explain sequence, provide feedback, and create mood or experience.

Story job terms

Story job asks what the element helps the viewer do. One element can have more than one job, but a primary job keeps the design focused.

Reader task
Uncover hidden, layered, collapsed, or attached information. Common forms include accordions, hotspots, annotations, flip cards, modals, and tooltips.
Let viewers investigate a set of material. Common forms include searchable cards, filterable databases, browsable galleries, maps, and collections.
Help viewers understand differences or similarities. Common forms include before/after sliders, toggles, tabs, side-by-side cards, and sortable comparisons.
Help viewers understand how something works, why something happens, or how parts fit together. Common forms include steppers, scrolly sequences, annotation graphics, and animated explainers.
Help viewers understand where something happens and why geography or spatial position matters. Common forms include locator maps, guided maps, scrolly maps, and layered maps.
Let viewers contribute choices or input that shape the result. Common forms include quizzes, calculators, decision paths, polls, and personalized outputs.
Help viewers feel, move through, or perceive something from a particular point of view. Common forms include 360 scenes, 3D viewers, audio-guided sequences, and immersive scrolly sections.

Levels of viewer agency

Agency describes how much control the viewer has. The right amount depends on the story need.

Control level
NoneThe viewer watches. Example: looping animation or video-like explainer.
GuidedThe viewer advances through a designed sequence, such as a stepper, scrolly, slideshow, or timeline.
Limited choiceThe viewer chooses among a small set of options, such as tabs, toggles, or category buttons.
ExploratoryThe viewer can search, filter, sort, pan, zoom, browse, or inspect.
ParticipatoryThe viewer contributes information or makes choices that shape the result.
HybridThe piece combines guided explanation with exploratory or participatory sections.

Forms are chosen last

The form is the specific structure that delivers the purpose, affordance, story job, and agency level.

Do not start here

Pattern Choice-Making Example: Monarch Migration Route

This example shows how a purpose statement turns into pattern decisions. The point is not “make a map” first. The point is to decide what the viewer needs to understand, then choose the form that best creates that understanding.

Purpose → pattern

Start with the purpose

Information: where monarch butterflies travel during migration, including major movement paths between breeding areas in the United States and Canada and overwintering sites in Mexico.

Takeaway / Experience: the viewer should understand that monarch migration is a multi-generational journey spanning thousands of miles. The experience should convey scale, time, connection, consequence, and fragility.

Choose the story job

Locate: help the viewer understand where the migration happens and why geography matters to the story.

Explain: help the viewer understand how threats in different regions can affect the overall migration.

Choose the agency level

A good fit would be guided, with possible limited choice. The viewer should move through the migration path in a designed sequence, with selected stops that explain specific challenges in the geographic trip and timeline.

This keeps the interaction focused. The viewer gets some control, but the story still guides attention toward the main takeaway.

Possible forms

Guided map

The viewer moves step-by-step along the migration path, with stops for breeding areas, migration corridors, threats, and overwintering sites.

Scrolly map

As the viewer scrolls, the map moves through the route and reveals key moments, regions, and challenges.

Animated explainer

The migration path animates across the map while short text explains what is happening at each stage.

Timeline-controlled map

A timeline controls the map, showing where monarchs are during different stages of the migration cycle.

Layered interactive map

The viewer turns layers on and off to see habitat loss, weather risk, pesticide exposure, or overwintering threats along the route.

Carousel or flip/hover cards

A sequence of cards shows important places along the route, with a small map, photo, and explanation for each stop.

Production

Production covers the practical responsibility of building a web-based interactive. Students should know the purpose and pattern before they start building.

First production question: where is the page built?

The build environment shapes what is easy, what is possible, and what the student is responsible for.

Build context

Storytelling builder

Tilda, Vev, Shorthand, and similar platforms designed for visual storytelling pages.

General builder

Wix, WordPress, Squarespace, and similar site-building systems.

Code-based

Plain HTML/CSS/JavaScript, React, Vue, Svelte, or another code framework.

Build approaches: control vs. responsibility

More control usually means more responsibility. The best choice is not always the most powerful choice.

Trade-off
Production choiceControlResponsibilityBest for
Native BlockLowLowCommon patterns the platform already handles well.
Embedded BuilderMediumMediumData visualizations, maps, timelines, quizzes, or graphics built in specialized tools.
Custom Code BlockHighHighCustom behavior inside a larger story platform.
Hosted CodeHighestHighestFull control over layout, performance, responsiveness, and interaction.

The 5 A's of production responsibility

These are the practical choices students must make when producing an interactive web element. A builder may handle some of them, but the student remains responsible for the final result.

Production responsibility

Appearance

What it looks like, how it is organized, and how the attendant text frames the element.

Asset Optimization

How responsibly the media files are prepared and served over the internet.

All Devices

How well the interactive works across screen sizes and interaction modes.

Accessibility

How well people can use and understand the interactive across viewing, hearing, mobility, and device conditions.

Attribution & Disclosure

How clearly the project credits data, information, media, and meaningful AI use.

Appearance

Appearance is the design and framing of the interactive. It includes layout, typography, color, spacing, hierarchy, polish, and the words that help viewers understand what they are seeing.

Design + framing
Design includes layout, typography, color, spacing, hierarchy, and polish. In a builder, students may choose from themes or visual settings. In code, students control those choices through CSS.
Structural design covers organization, hierarchy, page patterns, and interaction patterns. For this course, clean and consistent structure matters more than decorative flair.
Expressive design covers visual language, emotional tone, cognitive guidance, and interaction behaviors. It shapes how the piece feels, but it should support the story purpose rather than distract from it.
Attendant text is the writing that helps a viewer read, use, and interpret a graphic. It can include the headline, deck, intro, instructions, labels, captions, annotations, tooltip or popup text, takeaway text, and source notes.
Students do not need elaborate expressive design. The target is clean, consistent structural design: clear hierarchy, sensible grouping, usable pattern variants, and consistent typography and colors.

Asset Optimization

Asset optimization means preparing files so the page loads responsibly. Media should be saved in the smallest useful version, not uploaded as oversized originals.

Performance

Images

Images should be resized and compressed for web use. Students should not use large original files when the page only needs a smaller display size.

Video and audio

Video and audio should be compressed and served in a way that does not make the page unnecessarily slow or heavy.

3D models

3D models should be simplified or decimated when needed so the viewer is not forced to download an unnecessarily large file.

Builder vs. code responsibility

Builders often generate resized or compressed versions automatically. With custom code, students are responsible for preparing and linking the correct files themselves.

All Devices

All Devices means the interactive should work across desktop, tablet, and phone sizes, and across different interaction modes.

Responsiveness
The element should adapt to different screen widths. Text, images, controls, and panels should not overflow or require awkward horizontal scrolling unless horizontal exploration is the intended pattern.
Desktop interactions often assume a mouse and larger screen. Mobile interactions rely on taps, swipes, scrolling, and smaller viewports. A hover-only interaction needs an alternative for touch devices.
Builders usually provide responsive defaults or settings. With code, students must build responsive sizing and test breakpoints themselves.
A working desktop version is not enough. The interactive should remain usable and understandable on a phone.

Accessibility

Accessibility means people can use and understand the interactive in different viewing, hearing, mobility, and device conditions. Builders may help, but students are still responsible for choices, labels, instructions, and testing.

Access
Alt text describes meaningful images for people who cannot see them. Use it for photos, charts, maps, diagrams, and figures. Describe the information the image provides, not every visual detail.
Video and audio need text alternatives. Use captions for video and transcripts for audio or long video/audio content. Viewers should still get the meaning without sound.
Use headings for section structure, buttons for actions, links for navigation, and lists for grouped items. Use the HTML elements in a semantic way.
Check font size, line spacing, and contrast. If viewers have to strain to read it, the design is not working.
Some users navigate without a mouse. They should be able to tab through controls, activate buttons and links, avoid getting trapped, and understand where focus is.
Motion can guide attention, show change, explain sequence, provide feedback, or create mood. But viewers should not be forced to watch motion to understand the story. Provide pause, stop, replay, next/previous controls, reduced-motion support, or a static summary when needed.
Do not rely only on color to communicate information. Add labels, icons, patterns, text, position, or shape. Color can reinforce meaning, but it should not carry meaning alone.
Tell viewers what they are looking at, what they can do, what to click, tap, drag, scroll, or select, and what the result means. If users have to guess how it works, it needs better instructions.
ARIA adds extra information for assistive technologies. It can help describe: Custom controls, Open/closed states, Labels, Instructions, and Interactive regions.

ARIA: when it helps

ARIA belongs inside accessibility. It is useful for custom interface states, but it should not replace real HTML elements.

Usually no ARIA needed

  • Real buttons
  • Real links
  • Headings
  • Paragraphs
  • Lists
  • Images with useful alt text

ARIA may help with

  • Icon-only buttons
  • Custom tabs
  • Accordions
  • Modals/popups
  • Carousels
  • Hidden or expanded panels
  • Interactive regions with instructions

Common attributes

  • aria-label: label for icon-only controls
  • aria-labelledby: connect region to visible heading
  • aria-describedby: connect instructions
  • aria-expanded: open/closed state
  • aria-hidden="true": decorative icons only

Example

<button aria-expanded="false" aria-controls="migration-details">Show migration details</button>

Attribution & Disclosure

Attribution and disclosure explain where material came from and how it was made. If students had to look it up, the source should be traceable.

Credit + transparency

Items to attribute or disclose

  • Data
  • Information
  • Images and other media, including audio and video
  • Use of AI when it plays a clear role in what the viewer sees, reads, hears, uses, or interprets

Places to attribute or disclose

  • Inline within the writing
  • Built into the title, deck, or subtitle of the graphic
  • Adjacent to the figure or media, usually directly below it
  • Credits at the end of the story for general background sources or broader process notes

Use information. Don’t copy text.

  1. Read the source and understand the information.
  2. Choose the details that serve the story purpose.
  3. Write the explanation yourself.
  4. Credit the source so the information is traceable.

Disclosing AI

Disclose AI use when it clearly shapes what the viewer sees, reads, hears, uses, or interprets. If AI helped make an image or text, disclose it near the element. If AI helped make the code work, credit it in the credits or end note.
